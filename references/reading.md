# Develop an understanding of the work

Use this reference after gathering material, and while revisiting it. Establish what happens, why characters act, how events are told, and what ideas and artistic force emerge from that telling.

These are background reading methods, not mandatory headings for the delivered explanation. Scale depth to the request; a brief follow-up does not require a complete rereading. See [research.md](research.md) for retrieval and [explanation.md](explanation.md) for writing. Behavioral effectiveness still needs independent evaluation.

## 1. Establish the material boundary

Record identity, medium, edition, and actual reading coverage before synthesizing. Keep a source and locator for important notes. When stable paragraph IDs are unavailable, use chapters, page titles, or identifiable wording.

| Material | What it can support | What it cannot establish by itself |
|---|---|---|
| Primary text actually read | Events, diction, perspective, rhythm, scene treatment within coverage | Specific wording or distribution in unread parts |
| Reliable synopses and bibliographic records | Story structure, relationships, edition facts | Original syntax, texture, precise quotations |
| Criticism, research, interviews | Context, interpretive leads, disagreements | Automatic primary facts or the sole correct reading |
| User recollections and model memory | Search leads and questions to verify | Verified details or direct quotations |

Research independently when the user provides no document. Missing primary text does not prohibit explanation: narrow relevant claims without pretending to have read the whole work. Keep originals, translations, and adaptations separate. Attribute observations about translated wording to the edition read unless checked against the original.

## 2. Connect the dimensions of understanding

Use these questions as needed. Not every work has conventional plot, an explicit ending, or equal emphasis on every dimension.

| Dimension | Establish | Connect to |
|---|---|---|
| Story | Initial situation, conflict, turns, outcome; event order versus narrative order | Changes in relationships and choices; delayed significance |
| Characters | Desires, constraints, dependencies, influence | Conflicts between wishes and action; relationships driving events |
| Tone | How the telling treats people, events, and values; continuity and change | Why an event becomes comic, cruel, tender, or solemn |
| Central ideas | Recurring tensions and complications of easy answers | How events, telling, and ending embody those tensions |
| Artistic highlights | Irreplaceable language, structure, images, scenes | What a conventional retelling would lose |
| Ending | Resolution, displacement, suspension, or reinterpretation | Earlier details it completes or changes |

Build a concise picture of the story and relationships, then deepen it through consequential scenes. Revise that picture as reading proceeds. In non-plot-driven work, follow consciousness, imagery, or voice instead of inventing a complete causal story.

Prioritize scenes where relationships or actions change, recurring patterns concentrate, the ending becomes intelligible, or narrative attention is unusually sustained. Do not select only dramatic moments or passages matching an initial theme.

## 3. Perceive and describe tone

### Separate three levels

- **Character emotion:** shame, pleasure, fear, attachment, and other experiences. Distinguish self-description from what behavior supports.
- **Narrative attitude:** closeness, distance, ridicule, admiration, or other treatment. Do not automatically equate it with the author's personal attitude.
- **Reader response:** possible discomfort, desolation, intimacy, or amusement produced by that treatment. Explain conditions rather than prescribing every reader's feelings.

A character's suffering does not entail a compassionate narration; a cutting narration does not cancel a desolate situation. Use these distinctions where supported; do not presume irony or conflict between levels.

### Begin with treatment, not labels

Select relevant observations rather than filling every category:

- **Diction and metaphor:** how are bodies, people, and environments named, compared, or objectified? Whose language is being used?
- **Syntax and rhythm:** what do pauses, delay, repetition, and abrupt turns do? Specific wording is required.
- **Perspective and distance:** when does narration enter consciousness, pull away, or interrupt self-explanation?
- **Scene allocation:** what is expanded repeatedly, and what is passed over? A few excerpts cannot establish whole-book proportions.
- **Repetition and variation:** how do actions, images, or relationships change across recurrence?
- **Response and omission:** is suffering acknowledged? Are emotional expectations met? An omission alone does not establish authorial intention.

Connect **specific treatment -> target -> effect -> scope** in a short note. Observe that narration moves from pain to a ridiculous physical posture before concluding that sympathy is interrupted. Do not prove a cruel tone by saying the language is cruel.

### Test continuity and variation

Distinguish dominant tendencies, local tones, and transitions. Revisit continuous material from early, middle, and concluding sections, especially changes. Typical excerpts establish their own effects, not automatically an entire book's tone.

A tender moment may deepen overall desolation or genuinely challenge a claim of uniform cruelty. Decide from context rather than absorbing every exception into the initial thesis. Without distribution evidence, attribute a whole-work characterization to reliable criticism or limit it to the material read.

### Resist imported expectations

Do not assume trauma leads to growth, art provides redemption, or victims preserve an innocent inner self. Do not reverse the bias by assuming tenderness is always false or every work exposes concealed malice. When sexuality, violence, humiliation, or sordid detail matters, explain its presentation and role instead of abstracting it away as desire or conflict.

Stronger adjectives do not demonstrate closer reading. Explain clearly while preserving cruelty or tenderness; there is no need to imitate a narrator's humiliation of characters.

## 4. Interpret action, ideas, and artistic highlights

### Character action

At consequential turns, examine:

> Situation -> what someone wants from whom -> fear, dependence, or practical constraint -> action -> another person's response or changed circumstances -> next action.

These are questions, not blanks that must be filled. Separate acknowledged motives, behavior-supported inference, and unresolved elements. Self-deception, contradiction, and impulse are possible but cannot serve as arbitrary explanations for missing evidence.

If despair alone does not explain an act of self-harm, examine dependence, intended aggression, the other's response, and earlier actions. Even an elegant psychological chain needs support for each important link. Do not diagnose from one act or confuse understanding harm with endorsing it.

For claims of an only choice or inevitability, distinguish a character's subjectively bearable options from paths the text actually excludes. An outcome does not prove it was the sole possibility. Explain clear causation directly; do not use ambiguity as a substitute for reading, or force closure when evidence is incomplete.

### Central ideas beyond theme labels

Identify a concrete recurring tension, such as how something a character seeks also constrains them. Do not begin by assigning labels like loneliness, oppression, or human nature.

Trace how relationships, turns, and narrative choices develop that tension, and how the ending responds. Connect story and form without making every detail obey one master proposition.

Separate ideas enacted by the work, character opinions, publicly stated authorial intentions, and the analyst's interpretation. Introduce theory and context when they explain specific features; do not let them erase contrary details. A work may sustain a question rather than deliver a moral lesson.

### Artistic highlights worth explaining

Choose what actual reading makes most consequential: a scene, recurring image, voice, structural decision, or narrative distance. Explain its operation, local force, and relation to the whole.

Use a substitution check: if the events were reduced to ordinary synopsis, what would disappear? If a paragraph survives unchanged under another title, add the work's particular treatment.

A highlight need not be pleasant. Awkward, tedious, abrasive, or repellent treatment may have artistic force. Discomfort alone does not prove depth; explain its function.

## 5. Coverage, counterexamples, and corrections

### Long-work coverage

Map chapters or story structure, read consequential continuous scenes, and revisit across sections. Keyword search locates passages; it does not replace reading. Frequency does not establish importance, and absence from a search does not prove absence from the work.

Record actual reading and source coverage. Synopses can complete the story map but cannot complete language coverage. Attend to openings, turns, endings, and passages resisting the initial interpretation. No fixed number of excerpts constitutes a whole-work reading.

### Test consequential interpretations

For judgments driving the explanation, inspect material most likely to challenge them: another voice, changed relationship, local tenderness or cruelty, or an unexpected action. Record whether the judgment survives, narrows, or changes, with a brief reason.

Do not manufacture alternative readings for completeness. When real competitors exist, compare their explanatory reach and additional assumptions. If no counterexample is found, record coverage rather than claiming uniqueness.

### Respond to user corrections

Determine whether the correction concerns a fact, psychological explanation, tone, or personal response. Verify facts against sources, compare interpretations through evidence, and acknowledge individual responses without promoting them into the only possible tone.

State which detail changes which judgment. User confidence, earlier assistant assertions, and repeated criticism cannot replace support. Neither reflexive agreement nor contrarian disagreement demonstrates independent reading.

## 6. Notes and completion criteria

### Background records

Use the [schema](../schemas/research-notes.schema.json) for story, characters, tone, ideas, highlights, ending, and unresolved questions. See [notes-format.md](notes-format.md) for field and CLI contracts. Each understanding entry follows this shape:

```yaml
id: E01
statement: An important fact or interpretation
kind: interpretation
source_refs:
  - source_id: S01
    locator: Chapter, page, or paragraph location
scope: The scene read, translation used, or overall story
qualification: A necessary limitation or competing reading, if any
status: supported
```

Record concise support and revision outcomes, not lengthy private deliberation. Attribute secondary accounts as secondary; source counts do not replace relevance and quality.

### Ready to write

For a complete explanation, check that:

- Main relationships, conflicts, turns, and ending are understood, or the work's departure from those conventions is understood.
- Distinctive scenes are available, including uncomfortable material important to the whole.
- Tone has an explainable mechanism; character, narration, and response are distinguished and variation examined.
- Central ideas connect events and form; artistic highlights can be explained concretely.
- Important facts are traceable; interpretations match coverage; conflicts are resolved or retained explicitly.
- Available material supports the requested answer; missing material is not silently invented.

These are semantic review questions, not mandatory sections of the essay. For a follow-up, check only relevant parts.

### Return to research when needed

Use [research.md](research.md) when identity or editions are confused, key scenes conflict, the ending is unknown, a consequential judgment relies on vague memory, or language analysis lacks actual wording.

If tools cannot obtain the missing material, preserve supported explanation, reduce unsupported specificity, and briefly state important limits. Do not search indefinitely, invent details, or replace the requested explanation with a verification report.
