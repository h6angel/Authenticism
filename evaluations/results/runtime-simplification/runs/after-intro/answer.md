The Gift of the Magi is a short story by the American writer O. Henry, collected in The Four Million. It tells how a poor young couple prepare Christmas gifts for each other: brief as it is, the story layers surprise, sadness, and humor. The following discusses the complete plot and ending. This introduction is based on the [English text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/2776/2776-h/2776-h.htm); the original answer used familiar Chinese forms of the characters' names.

The story begins on Christmas Eve. Della counts her savings three times: only one dollar and eighty-seven cents. The amount is precise because she has saved it bit by bit over months of shopping for food, enduring the embarrassment of appearing stingy whenever she bargains. Jim's weekly pay has fallen from thirty dollars to twenty, and rent alone costs eight dollars a week. Della wants a fine gift worthy of him, but there is never enough money.

Their household has two sources of pride: the gold watch Jim inherited from his father and grandfather, and Della's hair, which falls below her knees. Della lets down her hair in front of the mirror, hesitates, sheds tears on the old carpet, and then goes out to sell it for twenty dollars. She buys a plain platinum watch chain for twenty-one dollars. Jim has been using an old leather strap and sometimes feels awkward checking his watch in public; the gift attends to both a practical need and his self-respect.

Once the excitement of buying the gift has passed, Della becomes afraid. She curls her short hair and waits for her husband, silently hoping he will still find her beautiful. This makes her feel real: she is willing to part with her treasured hair but still cares about her appearance and needs her beloved's reassurance. Generosity does not automatically dispel insecurity.

Jim returns and freezes at the sight of her short hair. The story does not immediately explain his expression, so Della rushes to say her hair will grow back and asks whether he still loves her. Jim embraces her and assures her that her hairstyle will not change his feelings. Then she opens his gift: the beautiful set of combs she has long admired in a shop window. At last they are hers, but the hair to wear them with has been sold. Delight turns to tears, then to a tearful smile as she says her hair grows quickly.

Next Della eagerly produces the chain and asks Jim to bring out his watch. Only now does the second surprise arrive: **Jim has sold the watch to buy the combs.** Neither carefully chosen gift can currently be used. Jim smiles, suggests putting the gifts away, and proposes supper. The action returns to the stove and to the life they must keep living together.

What moves us most is that each person has really noticed the other. Della knows Jim's watch deserves a respectable chain; Jim has remembered her longing for the combs in the window. Each gift is closely tied to the other's most cherished possession. That is precisely why their matching generosity goes awry: to fulfill the other's wish, each sells the treasure on which the gift depends.

The mismatch creates comedy and sorrow at once. Calculated only in money and usefulness, this is a poor exchange, and readers may reasonably regret it. Yet as the gifts lose their use, they become evidence of love. Della feared that losing her hair would make her unlovable; Jim's embrace first answers that fear, and the combs and sold watch then reveal how attentively she is loved.

The ending does not make poverty disappear. Jim still needs a coat and gloves, money remains tight, and the inherited watch does not return. Hair can grow, but an inherited object may not be recoverable. This gives the warmth weight: their choices have real costs, and mutual understanding cannot solve every difficulty. Praising their love does not require readers to make costly sacrifice the standard of love.

O. Henry's way of making the story readable also deserves attention. Repeated money-counting brings us into Della's anxiety; the gray cat, fence, and yard color her life with dullness. Then her loose hair shines like a brown stream, while extravagant comparisons to the Queen of Sheba and Solomon give the poor couple's treasures royal splendor. Their little home suddenly gleams, making the later sale of those treasures feel heavier.

The narrator frequently interrupts and jokes, even inviting readers to look elsewhere while the couple embrace. The voice makes the story feel like someone telling it at your side: intimate, without pushing every feeling toward tears. Della's anxiety, Jim's silence, and the narrator's playfulness are different emotions; their alternation creates a light but poignant rhythm.

The surprise is carefully prepared. The story mainly follows Della: readers know she has sold her hair but do not know what Jim has done that day. His stare therefore creates suspense. At first it seems to express shock at her haircut; the combs explain his disappointment, and the revelation of the sold watch completes the symmetry. **The ending changes the meaning of earlier details**, so its power extends beyond surprise.

The Magi of the title are the wise men who brought gifts to the infant in the Christmas story. At the end, the narrator first calls the couple unwise children, then the wisest of gift-givers, comparing them to the Magi. The turn from foolishness to wisdom reassesses a gift's value: they have not calculated how to preserve their belongings, but they have shown each other their place in one another's hearts.

What remains is the pair of temporarily unusable gifts, Della's tearful smile, and Jim's suggestion that they make supper. O. Henry places the grand story of gift-giving inside a small apartment that cannot afford luxuries. Its tenderness takes shape within these concrete, constrained daily circumstances.
